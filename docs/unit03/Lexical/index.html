Scene Three

Three Ways Linguists Have Told This Story

Same words, three lenses. Flip between them — none of them is "wrong," they're just different camera angles.

Words are LEGO towers

Item-and-Arrangement says a word is just discrete little morpheme-bricks glued in a row. No transformations, no drama — just concatenation.

hero + es→heroes
web + ed→webbed
web + sling + ing→web-slinging
child + r + en→children Awkward but honest: English fossilized two old plural markers onto "child." Great example, terrible advertisement, for pure brick-stacking.

Words are recipes, not towers

Item-and-Process says start from a base and apply ordered rules — some rules add material, some just change it. No bricks required.

swing +[PAST]→apply ablaut (i→u)Result: swung. Nothing gets added — the vowel itself mutates.
go +[PAST]→apply suppletionResult: went. The whole stem is swapped for an unrelated form — the most extreme "process" of all.
hero +[PL]→apply default suffixationResult: heroes. Regular rule: add "-es" after a vowel-final noun like this.
web (N) +[V]→apply zero-derivationResult: web (V), no phonology changes at all — just a silent category switch, ready for further suffixing ("webbed").

Words live in a grid, waiting to be filled

Item-and-Paradigm ignores "how" entirely. A word is a cell in a table of {stem × features}. The FST's only job is to output whatever sits in that cell — regular or not.

Featuregoswingweb (V)
PRESENTgoswingweb
PASTwentswungwebbed
PROGRESSIVEgoingswingingwebbing

Notice: the table doesn't care that "went" is weird. It's just the PAST cell for "go." Paradigm theory treats regular and irregular cells as equally legitimate — just filled by different means.

The punchline: they were never rivals

Arrangement, Process, and Paradigm are three descriptions of paths through one finite-state transducer — written as rule pairs like C:C, ^:e, and PAST:ed — running in parallel, not competing. Scroll down to Scene Four, hit "Run transducer," and watch all three lenses light up together, arc by arc, for the exact same word.

Two quiet promises every word-building machine makes

Every FST we've drawn so far runs on an unspoken instruction-manual logic: 🧩 Isomorphism — "one meaning = one brick" (want plural? snap on the -s brick, never shared, never missing) — and 🔗 Contiguity — "the brick only snaps on the outside" (a marker sits at the stem's edge, never buried inside, never reshaping what it attaches to). Both promises sound airtight. Neither survives contact with real language.

🧩 Promise #1 breaks: three troublemakers

👻 The Ghost Marker (zero exponence)

The meaning is grammatically real — a listener hears it — but no extra sound shows up to carry it.

I hit the ball today / yesterday She cuts / cut the cake one sheep / three sheep

🇫🇮 Finnish does it too: ol-i-n "I was" adds a person suffix… but ol-i "he/she was" doesn't. 3rd-person-singular shows up empty-handed.

🎭 The Two-in-One (bundling / polysemous exponent)

One tiny piece secretly does two+ jobs — or one word bundles several features into a single unsplittable form.

Priya's laptop → possessive Priya's arriving → "is" Priya's finished → "has"

And the reverse: am packs 1st-person + singular + present into one word — no way to point to separate pieces.

🇫🇷 French lentement "slowly" has a mystery -e- mapping to no feature at all (an empty morpheme). 🇫🇮 Finnish -mme marks PLURAL and 1st-person at once, just like "am."

🎰 The Rival Markers

One meaning, a whole menu of competing pieces — cats / oxen / sheep / geese / children are all "plural," realized five completely different ways. This case gets its own full interactive demo in Scene Five — Disjunctive Affixes.

Patching Promise #1: zero affixes (let a feature attach to an invisible Ø marker), empty morphemes (let a syllable exist without carrying any feature), and feature bundling (let one exponent legitimately stand for several features at once) — three small patches, one working machine.

🔗 Promise #2 breaks: away from the edges

🎶 The Shape-Shifter (ablaut)

No suffix at all — the stem's own internal vowel morphs to carry the grammar.

sing → sang → sung ring → rang → rung drive → drove → driven mouse → mice goose → geese foot → feet

🕵️ The Secret Passenger (infixation)

English does this playfully: un-freaking-believable abso-bloody-lutely — not official grammar, but the mechanism (insert material INSIDE the word) is a real, obligatory rule in Tagalog:

sulat "write"patay "kill"
Actives‹um›ulatp‹um›atay
Passives‹in›ulatp‹in›atay

-um-/-in- always land right after the first consonant, splitting the root in two.

🧬 The Woven Root (root-and-pattern / templatic morphology)

The most extreme break: the root itself never moves — grammar is woven THROUGH it as a vowel pattern, not attached to an edge.

🇸🇦 Arabic r-s-m "draw" → rasām(a) "he drew" / rusim(a) "it was drawn" 🇮🇱 Hebrew g-d-l "grow" → gadal "he grew" / gdila "growth"

English's shy cousin: sing → sang → sung is the same trick in miniature — one fixed skeleton (s-_-ng), different vowels carrying different grammar. Arabic and Hebrew just do it constantly and systematically.

Patching Promise #2 — multi-tape transducers: give the FST several tapes running in parallel (one for the consonant skeleton, one for the vowel pattern) and merge them into a single word. For infixes, mark an anchor point inside the stem first, then let a second pass insert material right there. Still finite-state the whole way through — just organized more cleverly.
The bigger fix — the paradigm-based method: Item-and-Paradigm never promised isomorphism or contiguity in the first place. It just says "here's the {stem × features} table — output whatever fills that cell." Whether the cell is filled by a clean suffix, a fused portmanteau, silence, double-marking, or a woven root, the table doesn't blink. Scene Seven builds this out in full.

Scene Five

Disjunctive Affixes: One Grammatical Job, Several Rival Affixes

Sometimes a language offers TWO or THREE correct answers for the exact same grammatical job — which one surfaces depends on which word you're dealing with. Very common in inflection-rich languages (Russian, French, Spanish, German). Even English does it, quietly.

Even English has it: the past participle's three rival endings

VerbPast ParticipleEnding used
looklooked-ed
givegiven-en
putputØ (nothing at all!)

Same grammatical job — "past participle" — three completely different solutions. Native speakers just memorize which verb takes which; a computer needs an explicit rule (or class tag) for it.

Meet "karta" (карта = map): one Russian noun, twelve forms

English nouns barely inflect (map / maps). Russian nouns change their ending by case (grammatical role) and number — and which endings a noun uses depends entirely on its declension class.

CaseClass I: zakonClass II: kartaClass III: rukopis'Class IV: boloto
Nominativezakon-Økart-arukopis'-Øbolot-o
Genitivezakon-akart-yrukopis-ibolot-a
Instrumentalzakon-omkart-ojrukopis-jubolot-om

Four nouns, four completely different suffix sets for the very same grammatical case — this is disjunctive affixation at full scale. A declension class is just a group of nouns that all follow the same pattern of case/number endings.

🕸️ How an FST Sorts Out Rival Affixes

Every stem is tagged with its class number the moment it enters the lexicon — e.g. <karta +noun 2>. The network routes the stem straight to its matching branch, so the -a/-y/-oj, -Ø/-a/-om, -Ø/-i/-ju, and -o/-a/-om suffix sets never fight over the same word. Bonus round: quiet rivalry inside English itself

This isn't class-based routing — it's the Blocking Principle (Aronoff, 1976): a more specific rule, or an already-lexicalized word, blocks the general default rule for the same slot. Pick a stem to watch two candidate affixes duel it out.

Inflectional Classes & the Genuinely Hard Cases

An inflectional class is just a group of words that all fill the same grammatical cells (PAST, PLURAL…) using the same recipe. English nouns and verbs don't all belong to one class — click a word to see which class it's in, and which "broken ideal" from Scene Three is responsible.

Meet the Rule-Breakers

Even within a class, individual words can break their own class's rules in one specific spot. Every language has them — English: "child" → "children," not "childs." The good news: you don't need a whole new system for exceptions, just a small patch on the existing one.

soldat "soldier" — the partial exception

Expected genitive plural: soldat-ov · Actual: soldat-Ø

Behaves exactly like Class I everywhere else — except in one single cell of its paradigm, where it quietly borrows Class II's zero-suffix pattern. 11 forms out of 12 are perfectly regular; only one breaks rank.

pal'to "overcoat" — the indeclinable

Nominative, Genitive, Dative, Instrumental — all: pal'to

This noun simply refuses to inflect — every case and number maps to the same bare stem. It gets its own tiny class where every cell collapses to one unchanging form. Borrowed foreign words often behave this way.

brjuki "trousers" — the missing singular

Just like English "scissors" or "pants" — no singular exists at all.

Linguists call this a pluralia tantum noun: it only ever appears in the plural. A morphological analyzer has to know to simply never expect (or produce) a singular form.

Scene Seven

Paradigm-Based Analysis: Think in Tables, Not Chains

Stop building words piece by piece — instead, look up the whole table (paradigm) of forms. The old question was "what piece do I glue onto this stem?" — which assumes there's always a piece to glue on. The new question is simply: "what word fills THIS cell of the table?"

Compare GO's table to a totally regular verb: WALK

Form ("cell")GO's tableWALK's table
Base / Presentgowalk
3rd Person Singulargoeswalks
Past Tensewentwalked
-ing Formgoingwalking
Past Participlegonewalked

Same five cells, same shape of table — GO just fills three of its cells with surprising words. The table doesn't change; only what's written inside it changes. "went" isn't built from "go" — it's simply the answer sitting in GO's Past Tense cell. No affix was ever required.

↔️ Horizontal relations

Many different words fill their tables the exact same way. cat/cats dog/dogs book/books — hundreds of nouns share the identical "just add -s" pattern. Instead of writing "add -s" thousands of times, we write it once and let every regular noun share it.

↕️ Vertical relations

Two cells of the same word's table quietly turn out to be identical. For WALK, Past Tense (walked) and Past Participle (walked) match — true for almost every regular verb. For GO, they don't (went ≠ gone) — spotting that GO breaks the usual vertical pattern is itself useful information.

A family tree for words: say the rule once, only override when needed

English nouns come in several classes — regular (cat→cats, just add -s), the fossil "-en" class (ox→oxen), the zero-change class (sheep→sheep), and the vowel-change class (mouse→mice). Facts true for EVERY noun live at the top of a family tree; facts shared only by irregular nouns live one level down; each individual word states only what's truly unique to itself.

ALL ENGLISH NOUNS — default: plural = stem + "-s"
Regular class — cat, dog, book… (inherits the default, no override)
"-en" class — ox → oxen (override: plural = stem + "-en")
Zero-change class — sheep → sheep (override: plural = same as singular)
Vowel-change class — mouse → mice (override: plural = change the internal vowel)

This is exactly the DATR-style default inheritance real morphological analyzers use: sheep needs one small note, mouse needs one small note — everything else is inherited automatically, so nobody writes "add -s" separately for tens of thousands of regular nouns.
